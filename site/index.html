Era 1 · Deep RL from pixels

One track, mastered. Every other track, failed.

The project began with Linesight's Implicit Quantile Network: distributional deep Q-learning that sees downscaled game frames plus telemetry and picks one of 12 key combinations every step.

I made training portable between CPU and GPU, added self-healing game restarts, reward shaping and a learning-rate schedule, then trained it for 10.6 million frames on Hockolicious, locally and on a cloud VM.

It got fast: about 54 s on Hockolicious, finishing 5 of 10 greedy evaluation runs (best 54.06 s).

Then it met other maps.

CheckpointHockolicious (trained)5 other campaign maps

All 8 finishes at 10.6M frames came on one map, A03. Fine-tuning on six maps at once was worse: the training loss fell while the driving collapsed everywhere, Hockolicious included. The replay buffer filled with failed runs and overwrote what the agent knew.

End-to-end RL can learn one track superbly but needs far more data to generalise. So the driving went to a robotics stack, and RL was kept for what it could add on top.

The switch · A robotics stack

Sense, plan, act. Developed on 5 maps, finished 27 of 63.

  1. SensePose from the game · map matching on the official-replay line · virtual lidar

The game gives exact pose, so localisation is free. The hard parts are the map, the speed plan, and steering a car with digital keys. 64 of the 65 campaign maps ship an official replay; its path, resampled every 0.5 m, became the racing line. On it, the controller finished A05 for the first time in the project (21.58 s).

    Learning the numbers

    Hand-set constants, replaced by models fit to data.

    Every learned model is validated across maps: the maps it is scored on are never the maps it was fit on.

    Speed model

    An MLP on 18 track-geometry features (curvature ahead, slopes, crests, jumps) predicts how fast a pro drives each point. Data: 139,063 points from the official replays.

    hand ruler 0.33
    learnedr 0.81

    5-fold CV by map. MAE 34.1 → 11.7 m/s. Test maps, scored once: r 0.84.

    Grip limit

    A logistic model of "runs wide", fit on 32,210 cornering steps from this car's own logs, caps corner speed where pros drift and this car can't.

    Leave-one-map-out, 54 maps. Demand alone (v²·|k|) scores the same AUC, 0.754. Single run on test maps: wall hits 11 → 2.

    The models made the car faster and cleaner, but the held-out finish count stayed at 2–3 of 10 in single runs: each change flipped a map or two at knife-edge jumps. That set up the question for RL.

    Era 2 · Residual reinforcement learning

    RL on top of the stack: small corrections, learned live.

    A TD3 policy reads a 39-number state (track-relative pose, the stack's own command, lookahead geometry, the 13 lidar rays, wheel contact) and adds Δsteering within ±0.25 and Δtarget speed within ±8 m/s, every 50 ms. It is rewarded for progress and penalised for impacts, recovery and getting stuck. While a safety layer owns the car, it is switched off.

    Its output starts at exactly zero, so training begins at the stack's behaviour, never from scratch: live, 760 of 760 steps matched the base exactly.

    Three generations, each fixing what broke the last

    1. Before training

      A random critic would mislead the first updates.

      Critic burn-in on the base stack's own runs: predicted vs true return r 0.89–1.00.

    2. td3_v1

      The actor never moved: mean |a| ≈ 0.02.

      Diagnosed offline: the TD3+BC anchor outweighed the critic's gradient. Anchor weakened 10×.

    3. td3_v2

      White exploration noise averaged out through the digital-key adapter.

      Held noise, one draw per 0.5 s. It learned to finish C05, a training map where the base stalls at 9%.

    4. td3_v3

      6 training maps and noise-free physics: narrow data.

      20 maps (the 6 plus 14 drawn by a fixed rule, never dev or test), seeded steering noise, 200 live episodes.

    What the data says

    • No significant net gain. 16 pairs finished in both, 9 only without RL, 11 only with it (p = 0.82). With the map as the unit, p = 1.0. Completion and recovery improve, within the noise.
    • It reshuffles which maps finish. RL fixes B07 (2/10 → 9/10) and breaks C02 (8/10 → 1/10), 7 pairs to 0 each way (p = 0.016 each, ≈ 0.16 after correcting for 10 maps). td3_v2 makes the same trade, but td3_v3 was initialised from it, so that is consistency, not an independent replication.
    • The design can't see small effects. Seven maps never change: six never finish, one always does. A gain would have to be about +17 to +20 points of finish rate to be detected 80% of the time.
    • One run per map lied. In single deterministic runs, RL looked like a clear win, 4/10 vs 2/10. Ten noisy runs showed that was luck at knife-edge jumps.
    • Selected fairly. td3_v3 got its one test look only by passing a dev-map rule written before any dev result.

    Limits, honestly

    What it can't do yet.

    Six test maps never finish

    B13, C13 and D04 stall in the first 10% in every version; B04, C10 and D07 stall at the same spots. These look like line or geometry problems, out of reach of a ±0.25 correction.

    The car is slow

    Finished maps take about 1.4–1.6× the pro's time. Pros drift through corners at speeds this car and controller can't hold.

    The test set wasn't perfectly blind

    Nothing was tuned on it, but it was looked at seven times, and after v6 two test maps' failure logs were studied, which led to measuring gravity. A general physics fix, still a mild leak.

    No collision telemetry

    Contacts are inferred: an impact is a speed drop of more than 4 m/s in 50 ms, which also catches hard landings.
